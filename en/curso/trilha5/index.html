🧭 When a skill is worthwhile (and the anti-patterns)

The complete decision tree: repeatable workflow or one-off? Skill, CLAUDE.md, MCP, or subagent? And the three anti-patterns that kill skills before they even trigger.

What it is:

A skill is justified when there’s a repeatable workflow, team context knowledge, verifiable output, and a multi-step task. When ≥2 of these signals appear, it’s worth packaging.

Why learn:

A poorly applied skill becomes noise in context. Knowing the green flags helps you avoid creating skills that nobody triggers—and that clutter the catalog.

Key concepts:

repeatable workflow · contextual knowledge · verifiable output · multi-step

What it is:

A one-off task, something the model already does well on its own, or a simple one-step query doesn't need a skill. A skill here is over-engineering.

Why learn:

Most bad skills are created for problems that didn’t exist. Recognizing the anti-case saves effort and keeps the agent lightweight.

Key concepts:

one-off task · model's native capability · one-step query

What it is:

Four tools, four purposes: CLAUDE.md for always-active rules, MCP to connect external services, subagent for isolated parallel work, and skill for knowledge that loads on demand.

Why learn:

Using the wrong tool is the most common mistake. A skill isn’t MCP, and a global rule isn’t a skill. The decision tree gives you the answer in seconds.

Key concepts:

always active vs. on demand · knowledge vs. connection vs. isolation

What it is:

The 800-line "everything-about-react" skill that tries to cover it all. The agent absorbs it poorly, the description stays vague, and the trigger never fires. Split it into atomic skills.

Why learn:

An enormous scope is the most tempting anti-pattern—it seems efficient, but degrades both the trigger and the quality of the response.

Key concepts:

atomicity · one responsibility · <500 lines · split by trigger

What it is:

Filling the skill with uppercase "YOU MUST" instructions and overly specific examples. The model memorizes the cases and fails outside them. Explain WHY and generalize beyond the examples.

Why learn:

Overfitting is silent: the skill passes your tests and breaks in real life. Understanding why makes the instruction robust.

Key concepts:

explain why · generalization · calm imperative · avoid overfitting

What it is:

The description is the trigger. If it's vague ("helps with code"), the skill never triggers — or triggers incorrectly, in the wrong context. Say WHAT it does AND WHEN to use it.

Why learn:

A brilliant skill with a poor description is invisible. The trigger is the only layer that’s always in context—it’s worth investing in.

Key concepts:

what + when · a little pushy · avoid under-triggering · explicit triggers

🚀 Publish, Version, and Measure

From a GitHub repo to skills.sh: folder structure, versioning via git, getting listed in the directory, measuring triggering, security, and the full skill lifecycle.

What it is:

A publishable repo has a folder skills/, and inside it each skill with its SKILL.md plus the resources (scripts/, references/, assets/). Convention understood by the CLI and skills.sh.

Why learn:

A nonstandard structure means the skill won't be indexed or installable. The convention is your passport to the ecosystem.

Key concepts:

skills/ folder · SKILL.md · scripts/ references/ assets/ · progressive disclosure

What it is:

Skills live in git. Because they’re installed as symlinks, a git push + npx skills update propagates the new version to everyone who installed it. Good versioning matters.

Why learn:

You don’t just control your repo — you control the behavior of people who installed it. Careless versioning breaks other people’s agents.

Key concepts:

git as source · symlink · npx skills update · changelog

What it is:

skills.sh indexes public repos and shows install count as a social metric. It’s the showcase: of the catalog’s 39,366 skills, only 0.3% have more than 100k installs.

Why learn:

Discovery is half the game. Understanding the catalog’s power law calibrates expectations and naming/description strategy.

Key concepts:

install count · leaderboard · power law · 53,9M combined installs

What it is:

Does the description trigger in the right cases? You measure this with trigger evals: lists of should-trigger and should-not-trigger queries, plus the near-misses that cause confusion.

Why learn:

Triggering is the most overlooked KPI. Without measuring it, you don’t know whether the skill disappears in the right context or intrudes in the wrong one.

Key concepts:

should-trigger · should-not-trigger · near-miss · trigger precision/recall

What it is:

A skill shouldn’t do anything the user wouldn’t expect from its description—no deleting files, sending data, or running surprise destructive commands.

Why learn:

Skills run with the user’s trust. One destructive surprise burns the reputation of the entire repo—and ecosystem.

Key concepts:

no surprises · least privilege · transparency · audit resources

What it is:

Publishing isn’t the end: it’s publish → monitor installs and feedback → iterate on the description and body → republish. And where to keep learning: the INEMA.CLUB portal.

Why learn:

A skill is a living product. Its lifecycle wraps up the course and gives you a roadmap for where to go after your first skill.

Key concepts:

publish · observe · iterate · INEMA.CLUB

⭐ The Best Process Skills

The most loved skills don’t teach a domain — they teach you HOW to think and work. test-driven-development, the superpowers family, and the process skills pattern (rigid vs. flexible).

What it is:

A domain skill encodes “what to know about X” (React, Postgres). A process skill encodes HOW to work—regardless of the subject or stack.

Why learn:

Process has a larger total market and is what agents lack by default—so it dominates the top installs.

Key concepts:

domain vs. process · stack-agnostic · trigger by task type

What it is:

The most installed process skill in Testing (obra/superpowers). It requires you to write the failing test BEFORE implementing the code.

Why learn:

Changes the agent’s lazy default behavior: every feature starts with real test coverage, and “passes the tests” stops being theater.

Key concepts:

red · green · refactor · verifiable output

What it is:

brainstorming, systematic-debugging, writing-plans, and verification-before-completion — each changes the agent's behavior at a specific moment.

Why learn:

These are the model for atomic process skills that invoke one another — the opposite of an "everything-about-X" skill.

Key concepts:

brainstorming · systematic-debugging · writing-plans · verification

What it is:

The process skill spectrum: rigid (a fixed sequence that allows no deviation) vs. flexible (principles the agent adapts to the context).

Why learn:

Choosing the wrong point on the spectrum is an anti-pattern: rigidity where cases vary creates friction; flexibility with MUSTs leads to overfitting.

Key concepts:

rigid · flexible · mistakes are costly → rigid · value lies in judgment → flexible

What it is:

Correct the lazy default, work with any language/project (enormous total market), and the gains add up across all work.

Why learn:

Understanding why people love it tells you what kind of skill is worth creating—a well-made process skill outperforms the average of a mediocre group.

Key concepts:

fix the default · total market · compounding effect · Testing&QA

What it is:

The patterns that make a process skill well-loved: atomic, triggered by task type, explains why, and composes with others.

Why learn:

You don’t need to publish the next superpowers — just apply these patterns to your skill.

Key concepts:

atomic · stack-agnostic · explains why · composable

🛠️ How to Create: From Decision to Publish

The concrete path: the decision checklist before creating, structuring the repo, versioning with git, and the exact commands — npx skills add / update — to go live on skills.sh.

What it is:

Four questions (repeatable? context? verifiable? multi-step?) and the tree that chooses between skill, CLAUDE.md, MCP, and subagent.

Why learn:

Using the wrong tool is the most common mistake. The filter separates useful skills from catalog clutter.

Key concepts:

≥2 yeses → worth it · always-on vs. on-demand · knowledge vs. connection

What it is:

The convention understood by the CLI and skills.sh: a skills/ folder at the root, each skill with SKILL.md (frontmatter + body) + scripts/ references/ assets/.

Why learn:

Outside the standard = the skill is neither indexed nor installable. The convention is your passport to the ecosystem.

Key concepts:

skills/ · SKILL.md · frontmatter name+description · resources

What it is:

Git is the source of truth. Symlinks make pushes propagate to everyone who installed it—treat main as production, use a branch + PR + CHANGELOG.

Why learn:

You control the behavior of people who installed it, not just your repo. Careless versioning breaks other people’s agents.

Key concepts:

git source · symlink · main = production · CHANGELOG

What it is:

npx skills add ./local to test first, add owner/repo for production and update to pull the latest versions.

Why learn:

The symlink makes the test loop fast: edit the local SKILL.md and the changes show up right away. That’s how you iterate before publishing.

Key concepts:

add ./local · add owner/repo · update · with-skill vs baseline

What it is:

Public repo with the skills/ folder in the right convention; skills.sh indexes it and exposes the install count. name + description are the storefront.

Why learn:

Discovery is half the game. The same text that triggers the skill sells it in search — two birds with one stone.

Key concepts:

public repo · indexing · install count · naming sells

What it is:

Decide → structure → test locally → refine the trigger → version → go live → measure. The single sequence you follow today.

Why learn:

Bring it all together in an actionable plan—from the decision to a running install count.

Key concepts:

decide · structure · test · fine-tune · version · publish · measure

🚀 Advanced Tips: Governance, Security, and Scale

The professional level: internal skills, the principle of least surprise, versioning strategy, team rollout, measuring adoption, and keeping dozens of skills alive.

What it is:

Proprietary knowledge and team workflows become skills tagged with metadata.internal, installed only with the INSTALL_INTERNAL_SKILLS.

Why learn:

Prevents sensitive context from leaking into the public catalog and keeps skills.sh from getting cluttered with noise that's only relevant to you.

Key concepts:

metadata.internal · INSTALL_INTERNAL_SKILLS · explicit opt-in

What it is:

The skill never does anything the description doesn’t promise: no exfiltration, hidden rm -rf, or access to secrets without asking. Least privilege.

Why learn:

A symlink + update propagates to many people. One breach of trust can burn the entire repo and spill over into the ecosystem.

Key concepts:

no surprises · least privilege · audit resources · no malware

What it is:

Classify each change by risk. Changing the description may be a major change because it affects when the skill triggers for everyone.

Why learn:

At scale, pushing to main without a strategy creates instability. Versioning discipline separates what's safe from what's risky.

Key concepts:

patch · minor · major · description = trigger change · tags

What it is:

Centralize approved skills in a curated repo, pilot them with a subgroup before rolling them out to everyone, and communicate before each trigger update.

Why learn:

Sharing with a team calls for consistency and control—different from publishing to the open world.

Key concepts:

curated repo · pilot · communicate update · onboarding via README

What it is:

Two metrics intersect in a matrix: install count (discovery) and triggering (trigger precision). The worst case is lots of installs with poor triggering.

Why learn:

A skill at scale is a product, and products are measured. Each quadrant calls for a different action.

Key concepts:

install count · triggering · adoption matrix · evals

What it is:

Run evals in CI, audit trigger overlap, retire dead skills, and keep the body lean — so a portfolio of dozens doesn't rot.

Why learn:

One skill is easy; twenty are a product. And that’s where the course ends—from here on, it’s practice and the INEMA.CLUB portal.

Key concepts:

evals in CI · overlap · retire · lean · INEMA.CLUB

📏 2026 rules: the skill for 5.5 models

What was missing: degrees of freedom, third-person descriptions and their limits, hooks inside the skill, the 5,000-token cutoff, not asking for reasoning, a checklist with a return path, and the 10-rule checklist with the validator. Wraps up the course.

What it is:

The basic rules in Anthropic's skills guide have been stable for about a year; what's changed are the 5.5 models, which follow instructions more closely.

Why learn:

According to the prompting guide cited by skill-creator-plus, older skills are often too prescriptive for 5.5 models—a hypothesis to test with your skill.

Key concepts:

stable rules · 5.5 models · too prescriptive · test

What it is:

How much you specify each step depends on how fragile it is: free-form text, a model with variation, or an exact script.

Why learn:

Lock everything down and the skill becomes rigid; leave everything open and the risky step is left to chance. The test: “What if the agent does this another way?”

Key concepts:

high · medium · low · narrow bridge × open field

What it is:

The description goes into the system prompt: third person, what it does, and “Use when…,” up to 1,024 characters.

Why learn:

Claude Code truncates description + when_to_use at 1,536 characters in the listing; anything beyond that isn’t read by the agent.

Key concepts:

third person · Use when · 1.024 · 1.536 · when_to_use

What it is:

A skill can declare hooks in its own frontmatter; they log when it's called.

Why learn:

A rule that must not be broken needs to apply every time, and a hook is always followed, through the end of the session.

Key concepts:

hooks: · PreToolUse · once: true · hard rule

What it is:

After compaction, Claude Code reattaches only the first 5,000 tokens of each skill.

Why learn:

An important rule at the end of a long SKILL.md gets lost in a long session.

Key concepts:

order = importance · 5,000 tokens · permanent instructions

What it is:

Asking it to write out its reasoning may be refused by 5.5 models (reasoning_extraction).

Why learn:

The skill stops halfway; asking for the answer, a short explanation, or a summary of the actions fixes it.

Key concepts:

reasoning_extraction · result · summary of actions · give the reason

What it is:

A list the agent copies into its response and checks off, with “done when…” for each step.

Why learn:

Prevents skipped steps and declaring success too early; the fallback line says what to do when the check fails.

Key concepts:

checklist · done when · return to step X · verification loop

What it is:

auditar-skills (a mirror of skill-creator-plus, MIT) includes a Python validator that checks all your skills in one second.

Why learn:

On a real machine: 123 skills, 358 errors, only 15 clean. Measure before rewriting.

Key concepts:

validate_skill.py --all · ST5 · ST4 · DS3 · five at a time
